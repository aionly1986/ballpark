## How a personal injury settlement is calculated

Most personal injury settlements start from a simple idea: add up what the injury
cost you, then account for what it put you through. Insurers and attorneys call the
first part **economic damages**: your medical bills, the future care you will still
need, and the income you lost while you could not work. The second part,
**pain and suffering**, is harder to price, so it is usually estimated by multiplying
your economic damages by a factor that reflects how serious the injury is.

This calculator uses that same damages-and-multiplier method to produce a
low-to-high **range**, then reduces it if you were partly at fault. It is meant to
give you an honest ballpark in seconds, not a substitute for advice from a lawyer who
can review your evidence.

## A worked example

Say you were hurt in a rear-end collision. You have **$12,000** in medical bills so
far, expect about **$3,000** in future physical therapy, and missed **$5,000** in
wages. That is **$20,000** in economic damages. If the injury is *moderate* (real,
lasting, but not permanent), a multiplier of around **3** puts pain and suffering near
**$60,000**. Combined, the subtotal is **$80,000**.

If the other driver was entirely at fault, the estimate stays near that figure and the
calculator shows a range around it. If you were **partially** at fault, comparative
negligence trims it: a 25% share of blame would drop the midpoint to about **$60,000**
before the range is applied.

## What affects your settlement the most

- **Injury severity and permanence.** A permanent or disabling injury moves the
  multiplier up sharply and often matters more than the raw dollar amount of bills.
- **Clear liability.** The stronger the proof that someone else caused it, the less
  your recovery gets discounted for fault.
- **Documentation.** Consistent medical records, wage statements, and photos raise the
  credible value of a claim.
- **Insurance policy limits.** A settlement rarely exceeds the at-fault party's
  coverage unless there are other sources to pursue.
- **Where you live.** State law on fault and damages caps can change the outcome
  significantly.

## State notes

States handle shared fault differently. Most use **comparative negligence**, reducing
your recovery by your percentage of fault. A handful of *contributory negligence*
states can bar recovery entirely if you were even slightly at fault. Some states also
**cap** non-economic (pain and suffering) damages, and each sets its own **statute of
limitations** (often one to three years), after which a claim is usually lost. Pick
your state above so a reviewing attorney can factor these in.
